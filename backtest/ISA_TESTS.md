# Testing the ISA dip rule (2017-01-31 to 2026-11-20)

Generated by `python -m backtest.isa_tests` on 2026-11-22. The rule (RECOMMENDED in backtest/isa.py): half the ISA in LQQ3 (Nasdaq-100 3x) and half in 3LUS (S&P 500 3x); buy a fund at the London open after its index (QQQ / SPY) closes in the bottom 10% of the day's range (IBS < 0.1), sell at the London open after the index closes above its 5-day average. Real London prices in pence (Yahoo, cleaned as in backtest/isa.py), 10 bps a side, sterling interest on cash, the S&P 500 in pounds as the benchmark. Index data: Alpaca daily bars, and 30-minute bars since 2016 for the hourly tests. Changes are judged on 2017-21 and checked on 2022-26.

## Summary

- **The rule:** 30.0% a year (38.3% in 2017-21, 22.2% in 2022-26) against 13.1% for the S&P 500 in pounds; max drawdown -33%, invested 20% of the time, 70% of trades won.
- **The edge is real:** after QQQ closes in the bottom 10% of its range, the next 3 days average +0.80% against +0.23% for any 3 days (t = 3.6), and that extra return was positive in 11 of 11 years. The same time in the market at random times made 2.8% a year (median of 500 runs); none reached the rule's 30.0%.
- **Expect a wide range:** resampling the daily returns gives 16% to 49% a year and a max drawdown of -25% to -52% (5th to 95th percentile); it beat the S&P 500 in 98% of the resamples.
- **The settings are not a lucky pick:** the 5-day exit is the best of 3, 5, 7, 10 days at 5 of 5 entry thresholds over the whole period (and in 9 of 10 threshold-halves); with it, the best entries are IBS < 0.15 (31.3% a year) and IBS < 0.1 (30.0%). Choosing the best setting each year from the earlier years only (walk-forward) made 36.8% a year from 2019, against 37.6% for the fixed rule.
- **No refinement beat it on both return and risk.** Of 27 one-at-a-time changes, 1 did better in both halves (Exit: RSI(2) above 70), with a max drawdown of -43% against -33%. Stop losses, time stops, trend and volatility filters, smaller positions in volatile markets and every hourly variant made it worse or only traded return for risk.
- **Costs matter most:** at 20 bps a side it makes 25.3% a year, at 30 bps 20.7%, at 50 bps 12.0% (37 round trips a year).
- **Verdict:** keep the rule as it is. Details and the reasons below.

### So why does it work?

What the data shows: when the index closes at the bottom of its day's range, it tends to recover more than usual over the next 3-4 days, and then the extra return stops. It depends on the close being at the low (not on the size of the fall), it shows up in every kind of market and nearly every year, and it is strongest in the Nasdaq-100.

The likely reason (consistent with these facts, but not something the prices can prove): a lot of trading has to happen at the close whatever the price. Index funds and ETFs trade in the closing auction; leveraged and inverse ETFs such as TQQQ and SQQQ must rebalance at the close and so sell after a falling day; options dealers sell into falls to stay hedged; stop orders and margin calls add to it. A day that ends with sellers in control pushes the close below where buyers would normally meet it. Once that pressure is gone the next day, the price drifts back. The rule is paid for buying from people who have to sell. That fits two more facts: it is quick (the overshoot is repaid within days), and it works in indices, where these flows are, far better than in single stocks, where a fall is more often news (backtest/DAILY.md: the same dip rules on the 500 largest stocks made a median 4.7% a year, even with survivorship bias in their favour).

Why the 3x funds: in the index the edge is small (about +0.6% over 3 days for QQQ); three times that, after costs, is +1.6% a trade. Because the funds are held only 20% of the time, their daily-reset decay and crash exposure stay limited. Where it hurts: when the selling is not temporary, each new low triggers another buy. The deepest falls of the rule: -33% (Feb 2025 to Apr 2025); -32% (May 2022 to Oct 2022); -23% (Dec 2019 to Feb 2020).

## 4. Hourly candles

LQQ3 and 3LUS trade in London from 8:00 to 16:30 UK, so in your ISA the US hours can only be used in the two hours both markets are open (14:30-16:30 UK, 9:30-11:30 in New York). Hourly London fund prices are not in the approved data, so these tests use the index's 30-minute bars (Alpaca) to decide and the funds' real London close to trade.

Trading at the next open or at 4:00 (about when London opens) earns about the same; trading at the close with the closing price known would earn the most, but deciding at 15:30 instead picks the wrong days (section 1) and earns almost nothing. Selling at the first half hour above the exit line cuts the winners short, as in London.

## 5. Verdict

- Keep the rule as it is: IBS < 0.1 on QQQ / SPY at the US close, buy LQQ3 / 3LUS at the next London open, sell at the London open after the index closes above its 5-day average, half the ISA in each fund.
- Selling on RSI(2) > 70 instead made a few points more in both halves but fell further (-43% against -33%); a matter of taste, not a free improvement.
- Do not add stop losses: the rule buys falls on purpose, and a stop sells just before the bounce it is waiting for.
- Trade at the open and keep costs down: use a limit order near the fund's indicative value (or wait a few minutes after 8:00 for the spread to settle); 20 bps a side instead of 10 costs about 5 points a year.
- Caveats: the data starts in 2016, so the rule has not been through a long bear market like 2000-02 or 2008, when dip buying in 3x funds could have been very painful; Yahoo's London prices are daily only; tax does not apply inside an ISA, platform dealing fees do (about 74 trades a year).
