# Pairs trading (2016-12-30 to 2026-11-25)

Generated by `python -m backtest.pairs` on 2026-11-27 in 18 s. Short the stock that pulled ahead of its partner, buy the one that fell behind, close when they meet again. Alpaca prices adjusted for dividends, signals at the close, trades at the next open, 10 bps a side on each leg, 0.5% a year to borrow the short leg, no interest on cash. Returns per unit of capital with that much long and as much short. See the module docstring.

## Summary

- **Famous pairs mostly drift apart:** of 13 well-known pairs, only 2 had a price ratio that reliably came back over 2016-26 (augmented Dickey-Fuller test, 5% level). Coca-Cola gained +58% against Pepsi and the gap never closed.
- **The rule on all 13 famous pairs:** 9 settings made between -5.0% and +0.5% a year. The best on 2017-21 (120-day z-score, open beyond 2) made +1.7% a year then and -0.7% in 2022-26; before costs and borrowing +1.8% a year. It won 58% of its trades, but the losers were bigger.
- **Pairs picked by the data, without hindsight:** -0.7% a year after costs (+0.5% before), against 2.4% for T-bills and 14.2% for the S&P 500.
- **Verdict:** the idea is real (the spread does tend to narrow a little more often than not: win rates of 51%-63%), but in large US stocks in 2017-26 the edge was too small to pay for trading both legs and borrowing the short one, and a pair that splits for good (a company's business changing) wipes out many small wins. It was much more profitable decades ago, before many funds traded it (the classic study, Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst 2006, found up to about 11% a year above cash in 1962-2002, with smaller profits in its later years).

## Notes

- An ISA cannot hold short positions; pairs trading needs a margin account, CFDs or spread bets, whose financing costs would come on top.
- The short leg pays the dividends of the stock borrowed; the adjusted prices used here include that.
- Survivorship: the panel holds only companies still listed today, so pairs in which one stock collapsed (the worst case for this strategy) are missing; real results would be worse.
- No interest on cash: in a margin account the collateral could earn some, which would bring the results up to about the T-bill rate at best, still nothing for the risk taken.
